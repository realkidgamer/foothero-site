His Neck Was Broken. He Played On Anyway.

FootHero Stories · Bert Trautmann · 6 min read

In the closing stretch of the 1956 FA Cup Final, Manchester City goalkeeper Bert Trautmann dove at the feet of an onrushing Birmingham City forward, took a knee to the neck, and was knocked out cold on the Wembley turf. He got back up, played on, and helped City win the match. Three days later, an X-ray revealed he had done it all with a broken neck.

Trautmann's path to that pitch was as unlikely as the story itself. Born in Germany, he served as a paratrooper in the Wehrmacht during the Second World War, was decorated with the Iron Cross, and was captured by Allied forces in the war's final weeks. He was shipped to England as a prisoner of war and held at a camp in Lancashire — and when the war ended and prisoners were given the choice to go home, he chose to stay, settling nearby and playing goalkeeper for a non-league side, St Helens Town, largely for the love of the game.

His performances were good enough that Manchester City came calling in 1949 — and the signing nearly didn't happen. Barely four years after the war's end, thousands of City supporters signed petitions against fielding a German goalkeeper, and members of Manchester's Jewish community, still living with the raw memory of the Holocaust, voiced fierce objections. A local rabbi publicly urged fans to judge Trautmann on his own conduct rather than his nationality. Save by save, the crowd came around, and by the mid-1950s the man once picketed at his own signing had become one of the most popular players in English football.

That popularity was earned the hard way. Trautmann spent over a decade as City's first-choice goalkeeper, known for a fearless, acrobatic style that showed little regard for his own safety — a trait that would come to define the biggest match of his career.

With City leading Birmingham City 3-1 late in the 1956 FA Cup Final, Trautmann dove to smother the ball at the feet of an onrushing Birmingham forward and took an accidental knee to the side of his neck. He lay motionless for several minutes, then climbed back to his feet, visibly dazed, and finished the match — including at least one more crucial save that helped preserve City's lead. He played on. Nobody, least of all Trautmann, knew his neck was broken.

The pain didn't ease in the days that followed. When Trautmann finally went for an X-ray, doctors found a dislocated vertebra in his neck — a fracture serious enough that specialists later said a further significant knock could have killed him or left him paralyzed. He had climbed the Wembley steps to collect his winners' medal with a broken neck that nobody, including him, had known about. That same year, the Football Writers' Association named him its Footballer of the Year — the first foreign player ever to win the honor. He went on to make more than 500 appearances for Manchester City, and in 2004 was awarded an honorary OBE for his contribution to Anglo-German understanding through football — a recognition of a story that started with protest banners outside Maine Road and ended with a nation calling him one of its own.
