He Missed The Most Famous Penalty In World Cup History. A Decade Later, Rival Fans Gave Him A Standing Ovation.

FootHero Stories · Roberto Baggio · 6 min read

At the 1994 World Cup final, Roberto Baggio stepped up to a penalty spot with the whole tournament riding on it — and sent the ball sailing over the crossbar. It became the answer to one of football's cruelest trivia questions. It would have broken most careers. Instead, it turned out to be the opening chapter of one of the sport's great second acts.

Baggio nearly didn't have a career to break in the first place. In 1985, playing for Vicenza in Italy's lower divisions, he suffered a severe knee injury that required major surgery and a long, uncertain recovery — the kind that ends promising careers before they properly start. He came back anyway, joined Fiorentina, and grew into one of the most gifted players in Italy, known for his close control, his vision, and a ponytail distinctive enough to earn him the nickname "Il Divin Codino" — the Divine Ponytail.

At the 1990 World Cup, hosted on home soil, Baggio announced himself to the world with a mesmeric solo goal against Czechoslovakia, gliding past defenders as if they weren't there. That same year, Juventus signed him from Fiorentina for what was reported at the time as a world-record transfer fee — a move so unwelcome in Florence that Fiorentina's own supporters rioted in the streets in protest.

Four years later, at the 1994 World Cup in the United States, Baggio carried an underwhelming Italy team almost by himself. He scored the extra-time winner against Nigeria to keep Italy alive, the winner against Spain in the quarterfinal, and both goals in the semifinal win over Bulgaria — much of it while nursing a thigh injury. Italy reached the final against Brazil, and a tense, goalless 120 minutes came down to a penalty shootout. Baggio, still hurting, stepped up to take Italy's fifth and decisive kick.

He missed. The ball cleared the bar, Brazil lifted the trophy, and the photograph of Baggio standing alone afterward, hands on hips, staring at the ground, became one of the most reproduced images in the sport's history.

What happened in the years after is the part fewer people remember. Baggio didn't fade — he kept playing at the highest level for another decade, moving through Milan, Bologna, Inter, and finally Brescia, and became one of a small handful of players to score at three separate World Cups for Italy, including a penalty he insisted on taking again at France 1998. Around the same period, he began practicing Nichiren Buddhism, which he later said helped him carry the weight of that missed kick without letting it define him.

In May 2004, at 37 years old, Baggio played his final professional match for Brescia — on the road, at AC Milan's San Siro, the stadium where he'd once been a galvanizing star. When his manager substituted him off in the closing minutes, the entire ground rose to its feet, Milan's own supporters included, and gave him an ovation that ran on for minutes. It was a different crowd, on a different continent, a decade removed from Pasadena — but it was, in its own way, the footballing world finally saying out loud what Italy had believed about him all along.

He went on to spend years as a UNICEF National Ambassador for Italy, lending the same fame that once made him a punchline to causes for children in need. Baggio never got to rewind that World Cup final — nobody does. He simply refused to let one kick be the only thing anyone remembered.
