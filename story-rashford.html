He Grew Up On Free School Meals. Then He Made a Government Fund Them.

FootHero Stories · Marcus Rashford · 5 min read

As a boy in Wythenshawe, Manchester, Marcus Rashford relied on free school meals and food bank parcels just to get through the week. Years later, wearing the shirt of Manchester United and England, he used his platform to force the same country that had once left him hungry to feed a generation of children just like him.

His mother, Melanie, raised five kids while working multiple jobs, and there were stretches when the family leaned on vouchers, food banks, and the kindness of neighbors to make ends meet. Rashford has never hidden that part of his story. Football was his way out — but he never stopped remembering what it felt like to be on the other side of the school lunch line.

By 2020 he was one of the most recognizable young players in England, a Manchester United forward with a growing England career. Then the pandemic hit, schools shut their doors, and millions of children who depended on free school meals during term time suddenly had nowhere to get them. Rashford partnered with the food redistribution charity FareShare, lending his name and his following to a campaign that helped push tens of millions of pounds toward getting meals to vulnerable kids over the Easter holidays.

It wasn't enough for him to stop there. In June 2020, with the summer holidays approaching and free school meal vouchers set to end, Rashford wrote an open letter to every Member of Parliament, asking the government to extend the scheme through the break. The government said no. Rashford didn't let it go.

The country that once left him hungry started feeding a generation of kids like him.

Within a day of his letter going public, backlash built fast. Restaurants, pubs, and local businesses across the country began offering free meals to any child who needed one, all while the story dominated headlines. Facing mounting pressure, the government reversed its decision and announced a new fund to cover free meals for children through the summer break — a full turnaround from where it had stood barely a day earlier.

Rashford didn't stop campaigning after that single win. Later in 2020, when a vote to extend meal support through the following school holidays failed in Parliament, he kept pushing, and the government came back with further funding within weeks. For his work, he was appointed MBE for services to vulnerable children in the UK during the pandemic — an honor usually reserved for people twice his age with decades of service behind them.

Rashford never asked to be a policy campaigner. He just remembered being the kid who needed the meal, and he wasn't willing to let it happen to someone else while he had the platform to stop it. The country that once left him hungry started feeding a generation of kids like him.
