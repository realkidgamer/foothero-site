Born In A Monrovia Slum. Became His Country's President.

FootHero Stories · George Weah · 7 min read

George Weah grew up in Clara Town, a slum on the edge of Monrovia with no running water, kicking a ball made of rags because a real one was out of reach. He would go on to become the only African-born player ever to win the Ballon d'Or — and, decades later, the only former footballer in history to become a head of state.

Weah was raised mostly by his grandmother, who pushed him toward school and away from the trouble that swallowed up so many kids in Clara Town. Football was the one thing that was actually free. He played barefoot on dirt lots with older boys, and by his teens he was already good enough that word of him had started to travel beyond the neighborhood.

He worked his way through a string of Liberian clubs before a move to Cameroon's Tonnerre Yaoundé put him in front of the right eyes. Arsène Wenger, then managing AS Monaco, signed him sight-unseen off a recommendation in 1988 and later became something like a mentor to him — flying him to France, teaching him the professional game from scratch, and watching him turn into one of the most electric forwards in Europe.

By the mid-1990s, Weah was the best player alive. In 1995 he became the only man ever to win the Ballon d'Or, FIFA World Player of the Year, and African Footballer of the Year all in the same season — a sweep nobody from the continent had achieved before and none has repeated since. A year later, playing for AC Milan against Verona, he picked up the ball inside his own penalty box, ran the length of the pitch past half the opposing team, and finished it himself — a goal still replayed as one of the greatest ever scored.

While Liberia burned, its biggest star was quietly keeping its national team alive.

But while Weah's career was rising in Europe, Liberia was falling apart. Civil war gutted the country through the 1990s, and its football federation was left with essentially no money to run a national team. Weah stepped into the gap himself. He paid for flights and hotels out of his own pocket, covered equipment and camp costs, and put up bonuses for a team that otherwise had nothing to play for. While Liberia burned, its biggest star was quietly keeping its national team alive — not as a publicity gesture, but because if he didn't do it, no one else could.

When his playing career ended, Weah didn't fade into ambassador appearances and punditry. He ran for president of Liberia in 2005 and lost the runoff to Ellen Johnson Sirleaf, Africa's first elected female head of state — then stayed in public life instead of walking away, serving as a senator from 2014. He ran for the presidency again in 2017, won the runoff, and was inaugurated in January 2018, becoming the first former professional footballer in history to lead a country. He held the office for one term before losing the 2023 election to Joseph Boakai and handing over power peacefully, a rarer feat in the region than the presidency itself.

There is no other career that runs from a rag ball in Clara Town to the Ballon d'Or podium to a presidential inauguration. Weah's is the only one — and it's a reminder that for some players, the biggest trophy was never the one waiting on the pitch.
