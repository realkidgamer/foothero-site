He Was Burned In Effigy For A Red Card. Four Years Later, He Scored The Goal That Beat Argentina.

FootHero Stories · David Beckham · 6 min read

In the summer of 1998, David Beckham was, for a stretch, one of the most despised men in England. One moment of retaliation in the biggest match of his life turned a World Cup exit into his personal disgrace. Four years later, against the same opponent, in the same competition, he answered it.

England met Argentina in the round of 16 at the 1998 World Cup in St Étienne, France, on June 30. The score was locked at 2-2 early in the second half when Beckham, fouled from behind by Argentina's Diego Simeone, kicked out at him from the ground. Referee Kim Milton Nielsen reached for a red card. England played more than half the match with ten men, held out through extra time, and were eliminated on penalties after David Batty's spot-kick was saved.

What followed was brutal even by the standards of English tabloid football. Effigies of Beckham were burned and hung outside pubs around the country. Newspapers put his face inside crosshairs or a dartboard on their front pages, and one tabloid's back page reduced the entire squad's tournament to a single line about him. For a period he needed police protection outside his own home, and the abuse followed him to away grounds across the Premier League season that followed, with opposition supporters jeering the sending-off every time he touched the ball.

Beckham didn't disappear. He kept starting for Manchester United, was part of the squad that won the Premier League, FA Cup and Champions League treble in the 1998-99 season, and kept taking the ball in front of crowds that had made a sport of booing him. In November 2000, new England manager Sven-Göran Eriksson made a decision that said more than any headline could: he named Beckham captain of the national team.

Effigies of Beckham were burned and hung outside pubs around the country.

The armband came with a moment of its own. In October 2001, England needed a result away from home in their final World Cup qualifier to guarantee automatic qualification, and found themselves trailing Greece 2-1 deep into stoppage time at Old Trafford. Beckham, exhausted after playing almost the entire match in central midfield, stepped up for a free kick from around 25 yards and bent it over the wall and into the net, leveling the game and sending England to the 2002 World Cup as group winners.

Then came the rematch. England and Argentina were drawn together again in the group stage of the 2002 World Cup, meeting in Sapporo, Japan, on June 7. In the second half, Michael Owen was fouled in the penalty area by Mauricio Pochettino, and the referee pointed to the spot. Beckham, the same player sent off against this same opponent four years earlier, placed the ball down and scored. England won 1-0, eliminating no one this time — just settling a score that had followed him for four years.

Nothing about the 1998 red card was undone by the goal in Sapporo; the two moments sit next to each other in the record books, both real, both his. But the distance between being burned in effigy and captaining your country to victory over the same opposition is about as complete a redemption as football offers, and Beckham walked every step of it in public.
