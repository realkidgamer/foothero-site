An Earthquake Buried Her Country In Grief. Four Months Later, She Handed It A World Cup.

FootHero Stories · Homare Sawa · 6 min read

On March 11, 2011, a massive earthquake struck off the coast of northeastern Japan and sent a tsunami tearing through entire towns, killing thousands and triggering a nuclear disaster at Fukushima. Four months later, Japan's national women's football team stood on a pitch in Frankfurt, Germany, and did something no team from Asia — men's or women's — had ever done. At the center of it was a 32-year-old midfielder named Homare Sawa.

The scale of the disaster back home was almost impossible to process. Whole coastal communities had been erased in minutes. Hundreds of thousands of people were living in evacuation centers with no idea when, or if, they'd have homes to return to. Against that backdrop, a football tournament happening on the other side of the world could easily have felt beside the point.

Instead, the players of Nadeshiko Japan — the team's nickname, after a delicate pink flower said to represent the ideal of Japanese femininity — carried the disaster with them onto the field. Sawa, already a veteran of the national team stretching back to her teens, had watched footage of the destruction along with her teammates before the tournament began. Several players had friends and family in the affected regions. They talked openly about wanting to give the country something to feel, even briefly, other than grief.

Japan was not considered a title favorite. They had never finished higher than the quarterfinals at a World Cup. But they beat host nation Germany in the quarterfinals, then Sweden in the semifinal, to set up a final against the United States — a team that had dominated the head-to-head history between the two sides for years and had never lost to Japan in a major tournament.

The final swung back and forth all night. The Americans took the lead in regulation; Japan equalized through Aya Miyama. In extra time, Abby Wambach headed the U.S. back in front. With the clock running down and elimination minutes away, Sawa met a corner kick with a subtle, improvised flick of her foot and sent the ball past the American goalkeeper to level the match again at 2-2, forcing penalties.

They talked openly about wanting to give the country something to feel, even briefly, other than grief.

Japan won the shootout 3-1. Sawa, the team's captain, finished the tournament with five goals and was named both its best player and its top scorer. After the final whistle, the Japanese players unfurled a banner reading, in English, a message of thanks to the world for its support during the disaster.

Back home, tens of millions watched the final on television, many from shelters and temporary housing. The team that lifted the trophy that night didn't fix the damage the earthquake and tsunami had done. But for one evening, in the middle of the hardest year in the country's modern history, they gave it something to celebrate — and Homare Sawa, who had spent a career mostly unknown outside the women's game, became a name every household in Japan would remember.
