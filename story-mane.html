Redemption

He Missed The Penalty That Could Have Won It. Then He Took The One That Did.

FootHero Stories · Sadio Mané · 6 min read

Sadio Mané had already missed the biggest penalty of his career once. Given a second one, in the same final, on the same night, he stepped up and took it anyway.

Mané grew up in Bambali, a small village in Senegal's Casamance region, the son of an imam who wanted him focused on his studies, not football. As a teenager he left home against his family's wishes to chase a trial at an academy in Dakar, arriving with barely anything and no guarantee anyone would even see him play. It worked. He was taken in by Génération Foot, Senegal's best-known academy, and within a few years he was gone from the village for good — first to Metz in France, then to Red Bull Salzburg in Austria, a route that barely registered outside scouting circles at the time.

Southampton signed him in 2014, and by 2016 Liverpool had paid roughly £34 million to bring him to Anfield, a club-record fee at the time. Alongside Mohamed Salah and Roberto Firmino, he became part of one of the most feared attacking trios in Europe. Liverpool won the Champions League in 2019, beating Tottenham 2-0 in Madrid, and the Premier League title the following season, ending a 30-year wait for the club. Mané was named African Footballer of the Year and, more than once, was in the conversation for the Ballon d'Or.

None of it came with a trophy for Senegal. In 2017, Mané's Senegal side went out of the Africa Cup of Nations at the quarter-final stage in a penalty shootout against Cameroon — and Mané missed his kick. Two years later, Senegal reached the final for the first time in over a decade and lost again, this time to Algeria. A golden generation of players, Mané included, kept arriving at the final hurdle and kept walking away empty-handed.

The 2021 Africa Cup of Nations, delayed by the pandemic and finally played in early 2022, sent Senegal back to the final — against Egypt, again. Ninety minutes produced nothing. Extra time produced a penalty for Senegal, and Mané, the captain by then, put the ball down himself. Egypt's goalkeeper, Mohamed Abou Gabal, guessed right and saved it. He missed. And when the shootout arrived, he stepped up again anyway.

He missed. And when the shootout arrived, he stepped up again anyway.

Senegal won the shootout 4-2. The decisive kick belonged to Mané. He struck it low into the corner, wheeled away, and was buried under teammates before he'd even finished celebrating. It was Senegal's first Africa Cup of Nations title in the tournament's history — a trophy the country, and Mané personally, had chased and lost and chased again for years.

What Mané did with the money that came with stardom is its own part of the story. He funded the construction of a hospital in Bambali, the village he'd once left on foot, and helped pay for a school and other infrastructure there, reinvesting directly in the community that raised him rather than distancing himself from it. Teammates and journalists who've covered him for years describe someone who never adjusted his idea of what he needed to the size of his salary.

There's a version of this story where the missed penalty is the whole point — a cautionary tale about pressure and failure on the biggest stage. Mané's version has a different ending. He missed, in front of his own country, in a final decades in the making. Then he got the ball back and did it anyway.
