Resilience

His Village Was Emptied By War. He Grew Up To End A Decade Of Messi And Ronaldo.

FootHero Stories · Luka Modrić · 7 min read

In the winter of 1991, war reached the hills outside Zadar, and Luka Modrić's family lost almost everything. He was six years old, and for years afterward, home was a hotel corridor where he wasn't supposed to be playing football. Twenty-seven years later, he'd end the longest individual monopoly the sport has ever seen.

The Croatian War of Independence didn't spare his family. His grandfather, also named Luka, was killed by Serb paramilitaries near their home village. The Modrić household fled, and the boy grew up displaced, moving between refugee hotels in Zadar with his parents and sister while the war ground on around them. The family's old village, Jasenice, was never resettled — to this day it's a place people are warned to be careful walking through, because of the mines still buried in the ground.

There was no pitch, no academy, no coach scouting for talent in a hotel parking lot. There was just a skinny, restless kid who carried a ball everywhere, including — according to people who knew him then — to bed. A local coach eventually heard about the boy who wouldn't stop kicking a football against the walls of the Hotel Kolovare and asked to see him play. That was the beginning of everything that came after.

He was never the biggest player on the pitch, and for a long time that counted against him — small clubs, loan spells at Zrinjski Mostar and Inter Zaprešić before he'd properly established himself at Dinamo Zagreb. A move to Tottenham Hotspur in 2008 raised his profile in England, and in 2012 Real Madrid paid a large fee to bring him to the Bernabéu, betting that his passing range and endless work rate would hold their midfield together. It did, for thirteen years.

Real Madrid built a golden era around players like him, winning the Champions League six times during his time at the club — 2014, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2022, and 2024 — a run of European dominance with few equals in the competition's history. But it was a summer with his national team, not his club, that changed how the rest of the world saw him.

At the 2018 World Cup, Modrić drove a Croatian team of roughly four million people through three straight knockout matches that went to extra time, past Denmark and host nation Russia and then England in the semifinal, and into the country's first ever World Cup final. Croatia lost that final to France, but Modrić was named the tournament's best player and took home the Golden Ball. Months later, voters handed him the Ballon d'Or outright — the first time since 2007 that the award had gone to anyone other than Messi or Ronaldo.

The trophy that had belonged to only two men for ten straight years now belonged to a refugee from Zadar.

Modrić kept playing at the top level long after most midfielders his age had retired. He left Real Madrid in the summer of 2025 after thirteen seasons, given a standing ovation on his final home appearance, and moved on a free transfer to AC Milan — still lacing up his boots in Serie A well into his forties. The kid who once treated a hotel parking lot as his only pitch never really stopped playing the game he grew up with nothing but.
