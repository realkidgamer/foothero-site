They Called Naples Unworthy Of A Title. He Won Them Two.

FootHero Stories · Diego Maradona · 6 min read

In the summer of 1984, Napoli paid a then world-record transfer fee for a 23-year-old Argentine nobody could quite believe they'd landed. The club had existed for nearly six decades without ever finishing a season on top of Italian football. Diego Maradona didn't just end that wait. He ended it twice.

Napoli in the mid-1980s were not one of Italian football's glamour clubs. The money, the trophies, and the respect all sat in the north, with Juventus, AC Milan, and Inter. It was common, when Napoli traveled to play those clubs, for opposing supporters to mock the city with banners about its poverty — a running cruelty that made the south's underdog status feel less like a sporting fact and more like an insult the rest of the country enjoyed repeating.

Maradona arrived to a club that had finished the previous season closer to relegation than to a title challenge. He took time to settle, but within a few seasons he had rebuilt Napoli into genuine contenders, carrying a squad that was talented but had never once tasted the top of the table.

Then, in the 1986-87 season, it happened. Napoli won the Scudetto for the first time in the club's history. Naples shut down. Fans staged mock funeral processions through the streets for their oldest rivals, and for weeks the city felt less like it had won a football title and more like it had won an argument it had been losing for sixty years.

Naples had never won a Serie A title. Maradona won them two.

The 1987 title wasn't a one-off. Napoli added the Coppa Italia that same season, then reached the top of Europe in 1989 by winning the UEFA Cup — still, to this day, the only European trophy in the club's history. A second Scudetto followed in 1990, sealed with Maradona once again at the center of everything Napoli did well.

His time at the club didn't end cleanly. A failed drug test in 1991 and a growing list of personal struggles brought his Napoli career to a bitter close, and he left the city under circumstances far removed from the celebrations of a few years earlier. It complicated, but never erased, what he had built there.

Napoli retired the number 10 shirt in his honor, and after his death in November 2020, the club's stadium — long known as the Stadio San Paolo — was renamed the Stadio Diego Armando Maradona. Murals of him still cover walls across the city's old quarters, maintained by residents who never stopped treating him as one of their own. A club that spent decades being told it didn't belong among Italian football's champions has the two Scudetti, and the murals, to say otherwise.
