Two Headers Won France A World Cup. One Volley Won Real Madrid Its Ninth European Cup.

FootHero Stories · Zinedine Zidane · 6 min read

Zinedine Zidane learned to play football inside a fenced concrete court in La Castellane, a housing estate in the rough northern quarters of Marseille. It's the kind of detail that gets dressed up after the fact to sound more dramatic than it was. In Zidane's case, it's simply where the story starts — and it ends with him standing over two of the most replayed moments the sport has ever produced.

He was born in 1972, the youngest of five children of Algerian immigrants from the Kabylie region who had settled in Marseille before he was born. Football in La Castellane was played in tight spaces, against walls and fences, with little room for the ball to travel far before it needed to be brought straight back under control. People who watched him as a boy would later say that's where the touch came from — not from coaching, but from geometry.

He left home in his mid-teens to join a football school in Cannes, then moved on to Bordeaux, building a reputation as a midfielder who could receive the ball under pressure and make it look unhurried. By the mid-1990s he had a place in the France national team — a squad that, heading into the 1998 World Cup on home soil, carried more than just sporting weight. Its players traced their roots to Algeria, Armenia, Ghana, Guadeloupe, and New Caledonia among other places, and the team became a symbol in a country then arguing loudly with itself about immigration and identity.

The ball never touched the ground.

France reached the final against Brazil, the defending champions and tournament favorites, at the Stade de France outside Paris. Zidane, never known as an aerial threat, scored twice with his head from near-identical positions at the near post, both goals arriving from corner kicks swung in by his teammates. France won 3-0. That night, more than a million people reportedly filled the Champs-Élysées, and Zidane's face was projected onto the Arc de Triomphe.

Two years later, France won the European Championship as well, meaning the team held the World Cup and the European title at the same time — a feat only a handful of national teams have managed. Zidane was named the tournament's outstanding player, adding to the individual honors already piling up after 1998. In 2001, Real Madrid signed him from Juventus for what was reported at the time to be a world-record transfer fee, making him the marquee arrival of the club's "Galácticos" era.

The signing paid for itself in a single moment less than a year later. In the 2002 Champions League final against Bayer Leverkusen at Hampden Park in Glasgow, Roberto Carlos sent a high, looping, awkward cross toward the edge of the box. Most players would have taken a touch to control it first. Zidane met it on the volley with his left foot, at full stretch, and drove it into the top corner. The ball never touched the ground. Real Madrid won 2-1, claiming their ninth European Cup, and the goal is still shown today as one of the finest ever scored on that stage.

Zidane was named the world's best player multiple times over the course of his career, but the two moments that defined him were never really about personal awards. They were about a kid from a fenced court in Marseille delivering, twice, on the two biggest stages football has to offer — once with his head, once with a finish that still doesn't look quite believable no matter how many times it gets replayed.
